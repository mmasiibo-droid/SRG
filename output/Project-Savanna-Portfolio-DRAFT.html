Executive briefing

SRG's reported 23% cross-system customer duplication, five product identifiers, 11-day monthly reporting cycle, 8.4% book-to-physical stock variance, and delayed response to a stolen laptop are not isolated database defects. They are symptoms of missing decision rights, inconsistent capture, unowned reference data, fragmented integration, weak controls, and no trusted analytical layer. Hiring DBAs alone would improve availability and database administration, but would not decide which customer record is authoritative, change store signup behavior during outages, set retention periods, or establish who reports a suspected breach.

Adopt a hub-and-spoke, offline-tolerant coexistence design: store systems continue operating during outages; a small, centrally managed integration and master-data hub reconciles signed, idempotent changes when links return. Start with governance, encrypted exports, reference-data controls, and a batch warehouse. Add near-real-time processing only to narrow use cases with a measured business value. A small central team coordinates stewardship through existing roles; it does not replace operational ownership.

The first six months prioritize measurable controls and quick wins: stop unencrypted customer exports, establish an incident clock and access register, standardize product units and customer capture, automate a daily sales reconciliation, and publish a governed pilot dashboard. The proposed 18-month envelope totals UGX 480 million including contingency. Before committing funds, Finance must validate local quotations, recurring costs, taxes, connectivity, and the distinction between budgeted allocations and actual spend.

Scenario and evidence

SRG has 23 stores, SavannaShop, 180,000 loyalty members, mobile-money integrations, siloed POS MySQL databases, e-commerce PostgreSQL, a standalone loyalty database, SaaS CRM, HQ-only Odoo, local HR/payroll and spreadsheets, 14 supplier spreadsheets, and manual mobile-money CSVs. A1 — EDM diagnostic and customer-data lifecycle

EDM is the enterprise discipline for treating data as a governed asset: decision rights, shared definitions, quality controls, security, privacy, metadata, integration, retention, and accountable use. Database management is the narrower engineering work of operating schemas, queries, backups, indexes, access, and availability. DBAs are needed, but cannot alone resolve the duplicated people across the POS, web, CRM, and loyalty systems or decide whether a loyalty profile may be matched on a phone number.

Principles: business ownership; collection for a stated purpose; minimum necessary data; quality at entry and monitored downstream; offline continuity with later reconciliation; traceable changes; least privilege; retention limits; human review for uncertain identity matches. Score 1 (poor) to 5 (strong); weighted score = weight × score, divided by 100. Weights prioritize outage tolerance, affordability, and maintainability over central uniformity because 23 stores lose connectivity and the four-person IT team has limited engineering capacity.

These are decision hypotheses, not measured vendor benchmarks. Validate cost, bandwidth, and recovery assumptions in a 2-store pilot. Reject fully centralized POS writes because outages would stop sales; reject unmanaged decentralization because it perpetuates competing identities and product codes. The selected design keeps transactional autonomy at the edge and sends versioned changes to a governed hub.

Core entities and assumptions

The Mermaid ER model is docs/diagrams/operational-er.mmd. Orders belong to one store/channel and contain one or more order lines; a customer may be anonymous, and guest orders must not be force-matched. Payments are separate from orders because split tender/refunds are possible. Loyalty membership is zero/one active account per customer subject to product confirmation. Suppliers and products are many-to-many via a supplier-product cross-reference omitted from the minimum assignment entity list but required in implementation.

Assumptions that may break: (1) a “customer” phone is unique—shared family phones, recycled SIMs, or no-phone shoppers disprove this; (2) a product has one unit/category—variable-weight goods and packaging changes disprove this; (3) a store is a stable reporting unit—relocation, franchise ownership, or channel fulfillment can change its business meaning. Preserve source IDs and effective dates rather than baking these assumptions into a destructive merge.

Normalization and reporting trade-off

For an illustrative unnormalized line item (receipt, date, customer, store, product1, qty1, product2, qty2, payment1, payment2), repeating product/payment groups violate 1NF. Convert to one row per order line with atomic attributes and a line number; separate order header, product, customer, store, and payment entities. Then remove partial dependencies (line attributes depend on (order_id,line_no), not only order_id) for 2NF and transitive dependencies such as district name depending on store_id for 3NF. The available workspace files include small fabricated examples; no assignment-scale unnormalized extract is present. This is a worked pattern, not a claim that it exactly matches the available fixture columns.

One deliberate denormalization is a daily store-product sales aggregate in the warehouse. It reduces dashboard scan cost and supports stock planning; it is rebuildable from immutable transaction lines, carries a refresh timestamp, and is never the operational stock ledger. Reconcile totals to the source before publishing.

B3 — Customer master data management

Choose coexistence: retain source applications and local customer IDs while assigning a hub golden_customer_id, source crosswalk, provenance, confidence, and survivorship. A registry alone cannot resolve/update the loyalty process; centralized replacement is over budget and outage-prone; consolidation-only analytics does not operationally return a resolved identity. Start in consolidation mode for a two-store pilot, then enable approved cross-reference updates after false-merge tests.

Golden-record survivorship is attribute-specific: verified loyalty profile wins for explicit preferences/consent; CRM may win preferred contact only when recently verified and purpose permits; POS/web source values remain immutable observations and never overwrite verified attributes solely because they are newer. Name/address require source, timestamp, and confidence; address is effective-dated. No source silently overwrites consent. Keep every candidate value and the survivorship decision for audit.

Candidate matching scores: exact source-system ID crosswalk 100; verified phone 35; normalized name 25; email 25; district 10; date-of-birth match only if lawfully collected and approved 15; cap at 100 and penalize conflicting verified attributes. Auto-link only exact crosswalk or score ≥95 with at least two independent matching attributes and no hard conflict. 75–94 goes to a trained steward; below 75 stays separate. The percentages/weights are pilot starting points, not statistically calibrated probabilities. Measure false merge and missed-match rates against reviewed pairs before expansion. A wrong merge can expose purchases/preferences and transfer another person's loyalty benefit; provide reversible links, audit trail, unmerge workflow, and rights review.

During outages, permit a locally generated provisional ID with source prefix, device ID, UUID, and creation timestamp. Queue idempotent events; do not assign another customer's points until matching is confirmed. Politically, the store manager owns customer service and participates in pilot acceptance; centrally owned ID rules do not mean a cashier must wait on a live network.

The warehouse star schema is FactSales at order-line/payment allocation grain with DimDate, DimProduct (Type 2), DimStore (Type 2), DimCustomer (privacy-limited), DimChannel, DimTender, and degenerate source transaction ID. Separate FactInventorySnapshot, FactLoyaltyActivity, and FactFootfallAggregate avoid mixing incompatible grains. Loyalty/contact details remain outside general analytics.

Red-team critique of the proposed strategy

AI-assisted draft for individual review. The strongest case against this strategy is that its apparent simplicity hides an operating burden SRG may not be able to carry. Four IT staff, only two of whom write SQL, are already supporting 23 disconnected POS installations, an e-commerce site, a mobile loyalty application, CRM, Odoo, payroll, supplier spreadsheets, and manual payment reconciliation. Adding an integration hub, warehouse, Type-2 dimensions, identity crosswalk, security logs, and a governance council creates more components to patch and more failure modes. A six-month “quick win” can become another pilot that works at HQ but fails at remote stores. If the team cannot monitor retries, resolve schema drift, restore backups, and explain failed loads, the new platform may merely move spreadsheet work into unmaintained pipelines.

The hub-and-spoke choice also risks central political capture. A central customer owner may label a store's legitimate local signup “bad data” and prevent sales; a marketing owner may use the golden record to expand profiling beyond what customers understood. A council chaired by executives can silence stewards who report poor source data, while the IT team is blamed for defects created by incentives and rushed procedures. A provisional offline ID avoids blocking checkouts, but synchronization can still award points to the wrong person or replay a sale twice. The strategy's thresholds (95 for auto-link, 75 for review) look precise despite having no adjudicated training set. If management treats them as scientifically validated, automation could multiply identity errors at scale.

The cost estimate is another weak point. UGX 480 million is both a hard scenario limit and a budget that must cover licenses, cloud, staff time, security, legal review, hardware, and connectivity. The line items are planning placeholders, not vendor quotations. Taxes, implementation, support, exchange rates, replacement devices, data transfer, and recurring fees can consume the contingency. A managed catalog or cloud service may add foreign currency exposure and processor/transfer obligations. If the budget is cut or spent early, an unfinished hybrid estate could be less secure than today's systems.

Connectivity and power are not temporary nuisances. Buffering events at a store creates a local cache of customer and transaction data; that cache needs encryption, key recovery, expiry, tamper resistance, conflict handling, and support when a device is replaced. If these controls depend on stable internet, stores may bypass them. If they depend on local expertise, outages will produce unrecoverable queues. A nightly ETL job can also produce misleading totals if late transactions arrive after a stock or sales snapshot; fast dashboards do not fix inconsistent source clocks or incomplete returns.

Privacy controls can fail in subtler ways. Masked values are often pseudonymous, and stable IDs still permit linkage. A “coarse” footfall stream may become identifying when joined with a rare purchase or shift schedule. Retention periods are deliberately left for legal review, but delays in setting them could permit indefinite copies. The report cites comparative breach cases without treating them as SRG cost estimates; nonetheless, those examples can distort a CFO decision if local legal exposure, customer behavior, and recovery costs differ.

The rebuttal is not to ignore these objections but to make each a release gate. Do not expand beyond two representative stores until tests prove idempotent replay, conflict resolution, backup restoration, and staff acceptance. Keep source systems operational and make the hub rebuildable from versioned exports. Make matching thresholds provisional and prohibit automated merge until a reviewed sample shows acceptable false-merge rates across district/name groups. Use a council charter with published exception metrics and an escalation path outside the data owner. Stage vendor decisions against written total-cost and legal reviews; if funding is insufficient, defer the catalog, streaming, and broad cloud migration before deferring encryption, incident response, access review, or backups.

Some objections may still win. If remote stores cannot reliably protect and replay queued records, the correct answer is not to force a central real-time signup; keep provisional local identities and defer point transfers until review. If counsel cannot approve the EU transfer model, postpone that rollout. If the BI dataset cannot be reconciled to finance, withhold the Board dashboard rather than decorate uncertainty with charts. The strategy should be judged by measured error reduction, timely incident handling, restored service, and staff use—not by how many tools were installed.

Reflection on the Project Savanna portfolio

This project changed how I think about data work. At the start, it was easy to see the brief as a technology problem: connect several systems, clean the records, and build a dashboard. Working through the design and the available files made that view feel incomplete. A system can move data quickly and still produce a misleading answer. The harder work is agreeing what a record means, who is responsible for it, and what the evidence can honestly support.

One practical lesson came from profiling the files in the workspace. The customer, product, sales, supplier, loyalty, HR-schema, and footfall examples looked like the inputs needed for analysis, but the data README identifies them as fabricated test fixtures. I could calculate exact results for those files, including seven accepted sales rows and one ambiguous date quarantined, but I could not turn them into claims about SRG. That distinction matters. Numbers can look authoritative simply because they have decimal places or sit in a polished report. I now see provenance, denominators, and limitations as part of the result, not footnotes to add later.

The same discipline shaped the ETL diagnosis. The requested error log was not present, so there was no defensible way to name the cause of the assignment's broken job. The local PostgreSQL test did run successfully and could be replayed without creating duplicate facts, but that only demonstrates the test pipeline. The rejected ambiguous date and the expected access-denied result each have different meanings. Separating those cases taught me to describe what happened before reaching for a root-cause explanation. It is better to report a clear evidence gap than to fill it with a plausible story.

The data model also raised a useful question about identity. A matching phone number is not proof that two rows describe the same person. People share phones, change numbers, and sometimes choose not to provide one. A reversible crosswalk and a human review queue are less tidy than automatic merging, but they leave room to correct mistakes. I would rather preserve a little duplication temporarily than combine two people's histories without a sound basis. This is a technical choice with consequences for privacy, fairness, loyalty points, and the customer's ability to challenge an error.

The proposed store design asks for a similar balance. Intermittent power and connectivity are not edge cases if staff encounter them as part of ordinary work. An online-only design would make a store's service depend on conditions outside its control. Keeping local operations usable and reconciling signed, idempotent events later is more complicated, but more realistic. It also requires clear conflict rules and monitoring; “offline first” is not a reason to ignore data quality.

Privacy decisions need the same practical attention. A loyalty service may need contact details to operate an account, but marketing and detailed personalization are separate purposes that should be explained and controlled. Footfall counts may help with staffing, while joining movement histories to loyalty profiles would create a much more intrusive system. I learned not to equate technical ability with permission or necessity. Before collecting a new field, I should be able to explain its purpose, access, retention, and the less intrusive alternatives considered.

The dashboard work reinforced that presentation is part of responsible analysis. The local page now reads aggregates generated from accepted fixture rows, and its filters and return values can be checked. It is still only a demonstration. A useful chart should make its grain, time period, currency, and limitations visible; it should not make a small synthetic sample look like a business trend. I also learned to distinguish a working artifact from a published service. A local HTML page is not a governed production dashboard.

I used AI assistance while preparing this portfolio, so I also had to think about how to check work that can sound confident. Generated text and code need to be compared with source files, test results, and the assignment requirements. In this case, the scripts, PostgreSQL checks, and calculated profiles provide evidence for specific technical claims, while the legal citations and scenario-scale business conclusions have limits that must stay visible. My responsibility is not removed by using a tool; it includes reviewing its output and disclosing the assistance according to course policy.

For my next step, I would like to build toward an entry-level data management or analytics role, while learning more about which parts of the work suit me best. This project exposed areas I want to strengthen: writing reliable SQL, testing transformations, documenting lineage, and explaining privacy and quality trade-offs in plain language. Over the next six months, I can make that goal practical by completing a focused SQL and data-modeling course, building a small tested ETL project with a data dictionary, and reviewing a privacy impact assessment with a lecturer or mentor. I should keep examples of the work and ask for feedback rather than treat a certificate as proof of competence. If this career direction does not match my actual interests, I will revise it before submission; the plan is a starting point, not a claim about experience or qualifications I have not described.

The standard I want to carry into future work is simple: separate what is known from what is inferred, and make it possible for another person to check the difference. Good data management is not just a clean table or a fast pipeline. It is a set of choices that people can understand, challenge, and correct. For SRG, success would mean that stores can keep serving customers, analysts can explain the numbers they report, and customers are not made less able to exercise choice just because the business has more data.
